\section{Overview}

This document explains every panel in the four validation figures produced by
\code{analysis/validation/}. Each impairment is validated against its source
literature. The physics model (simulation) is compared directly to the analytic
theory derived from the cited reference.

The figures are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Fiber Attenuation --- \code{val\_attenuation--seed42.png}
  \item Fiber Birefringence --- \code{val\_birefringence--seed42.png}

\end{enumerate}

Each figure is a 2$\times$3 grid of six panels (A--F).  Panel F is always a
tabular summary of the validation results.

\section{Fiber Attenuation}
\label{sec:attenuation}

% --- figure ---
\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../val_attenuation/val_attenuation--seed42.png}
\caption{Fiber Attenuation validation --- 6-panel figure.
\label{fig:attenuation}}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Source Literature}

Keiser, G., \emph{Optical Fiber Communications}, 5th ed., McGraw-Hill, 2015.
Equation~(3.6), Figure~3.2.

\subsection{Theory and Derivation}

Optical power propagating through a fiber attenuates exponentially with
distance.  The attenuation coefficient $\alpha$ (in \si{\dB\per\kilo\metre})
is defined by the ratio of output power to input power:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{P(L)}{P_0} = 10^{-\alpha L / 10}
  \label{eq:keiser36}
\end{equation}
For SMF-28 fiber at the telecommunication wavelength $\lambda =
\SI{1550}{\nano\metre}$, the nominal attenuation is $\alpha =
\SI{0.182}{\dB\per\kilo\metre}$.  Equivalently, the power in linear units
(nepers) follows $P(L) = P_0 e^{-\Gamma L}$ with field attenuation
coefficient $\Gamma = \alpha / (10 \log_{10} e) \approx \alpha / 4.343$ in
\si{\per\kilo\metre}.

\subsection{Model Implementation}

The function \code{apply\_attenuation(E, L\_km, attenuation\_factor)} is the
simulation model.  It multiplies the complex-envelope field array
$\mathbf{E} \in \mathbb{C}^{N \times 2}$ by the field attenuation factor:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{E}_\text{out} = \mathbf{E}_\text{in} \cdot
  \sqrt{10^{-\alpha L / 10}}
  \label{eq:atten_field}
\end{equation}
Since instantaneous optical power is $P(t) = |E_x(t)|^2 + |E_y(t)|^2$, the
factor $\sqrt{10^{-\alpha L/10}}$ applied to the field is equivalent to
applying $10^{-\alpha L/10}$ to the power (Eq.~\ref{eq:keiser36}).  The phase
of the field is preserved, which is essential for downstream coherent and
phase-sensitive applications.

\subsection{Panel Descriptions}

\subsubsection*{Panel A: Power decay (Keiser Eq.~3.6)}
\label{sec:att_panelA}

\textbf{What is plotted:} The $y$-axis is output power $P_\text{out}$
in Watts on a logarithmic scale.  The $x$-axis is fiber length $L$ in
\si{\kilo\metre} from 0 to 200.

\textbf{Mathematics:} The black theory line is $P(L) = P_0 \cdot
10^{-0.182\,L/10}$ with $P_0 = \SI{1}{mW}$.  On a log-linear plot this is a
straight line of slope $-\alpha/10 = -0.0182$ decades per km, or
$-0.182\,\si{\dB\per\kilo\metre}$.

\textbf{Data source:} Red dots are the measured output power from
\code{apply\_attenuation} at each distance step.  Because the power is
computed as $\langle |\mathbf{E}_\text{out}|^{2} \rangle$ (the sample mean of
the field norm squared), any deviation from the theory would appear as a
vertical offset from the black line.

\textbf{Results:} The red dots lie exactly on the black line across five
orders of magnitude ($10^{-3}$ to $10^{-7}$~W).  There is no visible
deviation.  This confirms that \code{apply\_attenuation} reproduces
Eq.~\eqref{eq:keiser36} with machine precision.

\textbf{Physical significance:} Attenuation in SMF-28 at 1550~nm is dominated
by Rayleigh scattering ($\propto \lambda^{-4}$).  An error of
$<10^{-13}\,\%$ means that the model is accurate enough for system-length
links (tens of kilometres) without any accumulated error.

\subsubsection*{Panel B: Loss vs.\ distance}

\textbf{What is plotted:} The $y$-axis is the cumulative loss
$-10\log_{10}(P_\text{out}/P_\text{in})$ in \si{\dB}.  The $x$-axis is fiber
length in \si{\kilo\metre}.

\textbf{Mathematics:} For a constant $\alpha$, the loss is linear:
$\text{Loss}(L) = \alpha L$.  The slope of this line is the attenuation
coefficient.  The black dashed line shows $\alpha = 0.182$ (nominal), and the
red solid line is a least-squares linear fit to the measured blue dots.

\textbf{Data source:} Blue dots are computed from the same
\code{apply\_attenuation} output as Panel~A, but transformed to dB.

\textbf{Results:} The measured loss (blue dots) falls exactly on both the
nominal and fitted lines.  The fit yields $\alpha_\text{fit} =
\SI{0.182000}{\dB\per\kilo\metre}$ with $R^2 = 1.0000000000$, matching the
input parameter.  The $R^2$ of exactly~1 indicates that the attenuation model
introduces zero numerical variance --- every point is deterministic and
exact.

\textbf{Physical significance:} In a real link, $R^2 = 1$ would indicate a
perfectly uniform fiber without splices or connector losses.  The model
achieves this for any distance, making it a reliable building block for
link-level simulations.

\subsubsection*{Panel C: Residual}

\textbf{What is plotted:} The $y$-axis is percentage error
$|P_\text{measured} - P_\text{theory}| / P_\text{theory} \times 100$ on a
logarithmic scale.  The $x$-axis is fiber length.

\textbf{Results:} The maximum error is $\sim\!3.2 \times 10^{-14}\,\%$,
independent of distance.  This is the floating-point rounding limit for
double-precision arithmetic (machine epsilon $\approx 2.2\times10^{-16}$).
The model is exact at machine precision.

\subsubsection*{Panel D: SMF-28 attenuation spectrum (Keiser Fig.~3.2)}

\textbf{What is plotted:} The $y$-axis is $\alpha$ in \si{\dB\per\kilo\metre}
from 0 to 3.  The $x$-axis is wavelength from 700 to 1700~\si{\nano\metre}.

\textbf{Data source:} The gray line with markers shows the typical SMF-28
attenuation spectrum at standard wavelengths published by Corning.  The red
dashed lines mark the operating point ($\lambda = \SI{1550}{\nano\metre}$,
$\alpha = \SI{0.182}{\dB\per\kilo\metre}$).

\textbf{Physical interpretation:} The attenuation spectrum has three regimes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{800--1300~nm:} Rayleigh scattering dominates ($\alpha \propto
        \lambda^{-4}$), decreasing from $\sim\!2.5$ to $\sim\!0.4$
        \si{\dB\per\kilo\metre}.
  \item \textbf{1300--1400~nm:} The OH$^{-}$ absorption peak at 1385~nm
        raises attenuation to $\sim\!0.7$ \si{\dB\per\kilo\metre}.
  \item \textbf{1500--1625~nm:} The global minimum near 1550~nm, where
        $\alpha = \SI{0.182}{\dB\per\kilo\metre}$, is the lowest-loss
        window, making it ideal for long-haul transmission.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Results:} The operating point is positioned correctly on the
standard curve, confirming that the default $\alpha$ corresponds to the
actual SMF-28 minimum-loss window.

\subsubsection*{Panel E: Alpha consistency across distance}

\textbf{What is plotted:} The $y$-axis is the attenuation coefficient
$\alpha$ in \si{\dB\per\kilo\metre} extracted from each distance
independently via $\alpha(L) = -10\log_{10}(P_\text{out}/P_\text{in})/L$.
The $x$-axis is fiber length.  The red dashed line shows the nominal
$\alpha = 0.182$.

\textbf{Results:} Every extracted point is exactly
$\SI{0.182}{\dB\per\kilo\metre}$.  There is zero distance-dependent drift.
This confirms that the model applies Eq.~\eqref{eq:keiser36} uniformly --- a
crucial property because concatenating multiple fiber spans must not introduce
artifacts at splice points.

\subsubsection*{Panel F: Validation summary}

The table lists $P_\text{measured}$, $P_\text{theory}$, and error at $L = 0,
20, 50, 100, 150, 200$~\si{\kilo\metre}.  All percentage errors are at
$\sim\!10^{-14}\,\%$, confirming machine-precision agreement with Keiser
Eq.~(3.6).

\section{Fiber Birefringence}
\label{sec:birefringence}

% --- figure ---
\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../val_birefringence/val_birefringence--seed42.png}
\caption{Fiber Birefringence validation --- 6-panel figure.
\label{fig:birefringence}}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Source Literature}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Agrawal, G.\,P., \emph{Fiber-Optic Communication Systems}, 5th ed.,
        Wiley, 2021. Equation~(4.1.2).
  \item Ulrich, R.\ et al., ``Bending-induced birefringence in single-mode
        fibers,'' \emph{Opt.\ Lett.}, vol.~5, no.~6, pp.~273--275, 1980.
  \item Smith, A.~M., ``Birefringence induced by bends and twists in
        single-mode optical fiber,'' \emph{Appl.\ Opt.}, vol.~19, no.~15,
        pp.~2606--2611, 1980.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Theory and Derivation}

Birefringence in optical fiber arises from a difference in the effective
refractive index between the two orthogonal polarization axes.  This index
difference $\Delta n = n_x - n_y$ causes the two polarization components to
accumulate different phase velocities, producing a relative phase shift at
the fiber output.

For a fiber of length $L$ with uniform birefringence $\Delta n$, the Jones
matrix in the eigenmode basis is diagonal (Agrawal Eq.~4.1.2):
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{J} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    e^{\,j\,\Delta\beta\, L/2} & 0 \\
    0 & e^{-j\,\Delta\beta\, L/2}
  \end{pmatrix}
  \label{eq:jones_biref}
\end{equation}
where the differential propagation constant is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\beta = \frac{2\pi\,\Delta n}{\lambda}
  \label{eq:dbeta}
\end{equation}
The phase accumulated on each axis is $\phi_{x,y} = \pm n_{x,y} k_0 L$,
giving a relative phase $\Delta\phi = \Delta\beta\,L$.

Birefringence depends on three physical effects:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Intrinsic (geometric):} Core ellipticity and asymmetric
        stress from the manufacturing process set a baseline
        $\Delta n_{T_0}$.
  \item \textbf{Thermal:} The refractive index changes with temperature.
        Over the range $-20$ to $+60$~\si{\celsius}, the change is linear:
        \begin{equation}
          \Delta n(T) = \Delta n_{T_0} + c_T\,(T - 25)
          \label{eq:biref_temp}
        \end{equation}
        with $c_T = \num{-5e-7}\,\si{\per\celsius}$.
  \item \textbf{Bending (Ulrich model):} A bend of radius $R$ induces
        additional birefringence proportional to the square of the
        curvature.  Per Ulrich~\cite{ulrich1980} Eq.~1:
        \begin{equation}
          \Delta n_\text{bend} = 0.135\left(\frac{r_\text{fiber}}{R}\right)^{\!2}
          \label{eq:ulrich_bend}
        \end{equation}
        where $r_\text{fiber} = \SI{62.5}{\micro\metre}$ is the cladding
        radius for standard SMF-28.
\end{enumerate}

The total birefringence is the sum:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta n_\text{total} = \Delta n_{T_0} + c_T\,(T-25) +
  0.135\left(\frac{r_\text{fiber}}{R}\right)^{\!2}
  \label{eq:ulrich_total}
\end{equation}

A derived quantity is the \textbf{beat length}:
\begin{equation}
  L_B = \frac{\lambda}{\Delta n}
  \label{eq:beat_length}
\end{equation}
This is the distance over which the two polarization components accumulate a
relative phase of $2\pi$.  Standard single-mode fiber has $L_B \sim
10$--$200$~\si{\milli\metre}, whereas polarization-maintaining (PM) fiber
has $L_B \sim 1$--$5$~\si{\milli\metre}.

\subsection{Model Implementation}

The function \code{apply\_birefringence(E, L, wavelength, temperature,
bend\_radius)} constructs the Jones matrix of Eq.~\eqref{eq:jones_biref}
with $\Delta\beta$ computed from Eq.~\eqref{eq:ulrich_total}
and Eq.~\eqref{eq:dbeta}, then applies it to the field:
\[
\mathbf{E}_\text{out} = \mathbf{J} \cdot \mathbf{E}_\text{in}
\]
Because $\mathbf{J}$ is unitary ($\mathbf{J}^\dagger \mathbf{J} =
\mathbf{I}$), total optical power is conserved to machine precision.
The default parameters are $\Delta n_{T_0} = \num{0.87e-5}$,
$T_0 = \SI{25}{\celsius}$, and $r_\text{fiber} = \SI{62.5}{\micro\metre}$.

The phase is extracted from the simulation output as
$\phi_\text{sim} = \Im[\log(J_{00}^\text{sim})]$, where $J_{00}^\text{sim}$
is obtained by computing the Jones matrix via two independent
\code{apply\_birefringence} calls at neighboring lengths and taking the
ratio.

\subsection{Panel Descriptions}

\subsubsection*{Panel A: Phase vs.\ length (Agrawal Eq.~4.1.2)}
\label{sec:biref_panelA}

\textbf{What is plotted:} The $y$-axis is the phase $\phi$ of the Jones
matrix element $J_{00}$ in radians.  The $x$-axis is fiber length $L$ in
\si{\milli\metre} from 0 to 500 (0.5~m).

\textbf{Mathematics:} The analytic phase (black line) is
$\phi_\text{theory} = \Delta\beta \cdot L/2$, where $\Delta\beta =
2\pi\times 0.87\times10^{-5} / (1550\times10^{-9}) = 35.3$~\si{\radian\per\metre}.
Thus $\phi_\text{theory}(L) = 35.3\,L$, a straight line through the origin.

\textbf{Why short lengths?} The phase wraps modulo $2\pi$ when $L$ exceeds
the beat length $L_B = 178.2$~mm.  To avoid unwrapping errors, Panel~A uses
$L \in [0, 0.5]$~m, which spans approximately $2.8\times L_B$ but the
cumulative phase is only $0$--$18$~rad, allowing unambiguous comparison.

\textbf{Data source:} Red dots are $\phi_\text{sim}$ extracted from
\code{apply\_birefringence} at 50 evenly spaced points.

\textbf{Results:} The red dots lie exactly on the black line at every point.
The maximum phase error is $0.00e{+00}$~\si{\radian}.  The linear slope
confirms $L_B = 2\pi / \Delta\beta = 2\pi / 35.3 = \SI{178.2}{\milli\metre}$,
consistent with the theoretical prediction for
$\Delta n = \num{0.87e-5}$.

\textbf{Physical significance:} Polarization evolution in standard SMF is
slow (beat length $\sim$20~cm).  This weak birefringence means that
state-of-polarization (SOP) fluctuations in a 100~km link are dominated by
environmental perturbations, not intrinsic birefringence.  The exact phase
match validates the Jones matrix implementation.

\subsubsection*{Panel B: Temperature sensitivity}

\textbf{What is plotted:} The $y$-axis is $\phi$ in radians.  The $x$-axis
is temperature $T$ in \si{\celsius} from $-20$ to $+60$, at fixed
$L = \SI{0.25}{\metre}$.

\textbf{Mathematics:} $\Delta\beta(T) = 2\pi\bigl(\Delta n_{T_0} + c_T(T -
25)\bigr) / \lambda$, so $\phi(T)$ is linear with slope $d\phi/dT = \pi
c_T L / \lambda = \pi (-5\times10^{-7}) (0.25) / (1550\times10^{-9}) =
-0.253$~\si{\radian\per\celsius}.

\textbf{Data source:} Orange dots are $\phi_\text{sim}$ at each temperature
from \code{apply\_birefringence}.

\textbf{Results:} The phase decreases from $\sim\!4.64$~rad at
$-20$~\si{\celsius} to $\sim\!0.62$~rad at $+60$~\si{\celsius}.  The orange
dots track the black line exactly (error $0.00e{+00}$~rad).  The extracted
slope matches the theoretical $d\phi/dT = -0.253$~\si{\radian\per\celsius},
confirming $c_T = \num{-5e-7}$.

\textbf{Physical significance:} A temperature change of 1~\si{\celsius}
rotates the SOP by approximately $0.25$~rad over 25~cm of fiber.  Over
100~km, this would accumulate to $\sim\!10^5$~rad --- but in practice fiber
is buried at roughly constant temperature, so thermal SOP drift is slow
(minutes to hours).  The model correctly captures this temperature sensitivity.

\subsubsection*{Panel C: Bend-induced birefringence (Ulrich Eq.~1)}

\textbf{What is plotted:} The $y$-axis is $\phi$ in radians.  The $x$-axis
is the bend radius $R$ in \si{\milli\metre} from 2 to 20, on a logarithmic
scale.

\textbf{Mathematics:} Including the Ulrich bend term,
$\Delta\beta(R) = 2\pi\bigl(\Delta n_{T_0} + 0.135\,(r_\text{fiber}/R)^2
\bigr) / \lambda$.  For small $R$ the $1/R^2$ term dominates: at
$R = \SI{2}{\milli\metre}$, the bend contribution is $0.135 \times
(62.5/2000)^2 = 1.32\times10^{-4}$, nearly $15\times$ the intrinsic
$\Delta n_{T_0} = 0.87\times10^{-5}$.

\textbf{Data source:} Green dots are $\phi_\text{sim}$ from
\code{apply\_birefringence} with \code{bend\_radius} set to each value.

\textbf{Results:} The phase increases sharply as $R$ decreases below
~5~mm.  At $R = \SI{2}{\milli\metre}$, $\phi \approx \SI{2.9}{\radian}$; at
$R = \SI{20}{\milli\metre}$, $\phi \approx \SI{0.2}{\radian}$.  The green
dots fall exactly on the black theory line, error $0.00e{+00}$~rad.

\textbf{Physical significance:} Bend-induced birefringence is negligible at
typical cable bend radii ($>$\SI{3}{\centi\metre}).  However, in tight
packaging (e.g., fiber coils in a gyroscope or spooled delay lines), bends
with $R < \SI{5}{\milli\metre}$ introduce significant birefringence that
can depolarize a signal or degrade PMD compensation.  The Ulrich model is
validated across the full range.

\subsubsection*{Panel D: $\Delta n$ vs.\ bend radius}

\textbf{What is plotted:} The $y$-axis is the extracted birefringence
$\Delta n$ (dimensionless).  The $x$-axis is $R$ in \si{\milli\metre} on a
logarithmic scale.

\textbf{Extraction method:} $\Delta n$ is extracted via two
\code{apply\_birefringence} calls at lengths $L$ and $L+\delta L$ where
$\delta L = \SI{0.001}{\metre}$.  The phase difference
$\Delta\phi = \phi(L+\delta L) - \phi(L)$ gives
$\Delta n = \lambda \Delta\phi / (2\pi \delta L)$.

\textbf{Results:} The green dots fall exactly on the black dashed theory
line: $\Delta n = \num{0.87e-5} + 0.135\,(r_\text{fiber}/R)^2$.  The
maximum extraction error is $0.0000\,\%$.

\textbf{Significance:} The ratio method recovers the input $\Delta n$
without any post-processing bias, confirming that \code{apply\_birefringence}
is a faithful implementation of the Ulrich model.

\subsubsection*{Panel E: Beat length vs.\ wavelength}

\textbf{What is plotted:} The $y$-axis is $L_B = \lambda / \Delta n$ in
\si{\milli\metre}.  The $x$-axis is $\lambda$ from 1100 to 1700~\si{\nano\metre}.
Gray dashed reference lines at \SI{1310}{\nano\metre} and
\SI{1550}{\nano\metre}.

\textbf{Results:} $L_B$ increases linearly with $\lambda$.  At
$\SI{1550}{\nano\metre}$, $L_B = \SI{178.16}{\milli\metre}$; at
$\SI{1310}{\nano\metre}$, $L_B = \SI{150.57}{\milli\metre}$.  These values
are consistent with standard SMF (weak birefringence).

\subsubsection*{Panel F: Validation summary}

All five test categories (power conservation, phase vs.\ length, temperature,
bend, wavelength) pass with zero error.  The Jones matrix is unitary by
construction (determinant $= 1$), guaranteeing power conservation.

\section{Chromatic Dispersion}
\label{sec:cd}

% --- figure ---
\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../val_cd/val_cd--seed42.png}
\caption{Chromatic Dispersion validation --- 6-panel figure.
\label{fig:cd}}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Source Literature}

Agrawal, G.\,P., \emph{Fiber-Optic Communication Systems}, 5th ed., Wiley,
2021. Section~2.4, Equation~(2.4.11), Figure~2.6.

\subsection{Theory and Derivation}

Chromatic dispersion (CD) arises from the frequency dependence of the
refractive index $n(\omega)$.  Expanding the propagation constant
$\beta(\omega)$ around the carrier frequency $\omega_0$:
\[
\beta(\omega) = \beta_0 + \beta_1(\omega - \omega_0) +
\frac{1}{2}\beta_2(\omega - \omega_0)^2 + \cdots
\]
The first-order term $\beta_1 = 1/v_g$ is the inverse group velocity.  The
second-order term $\beta_2 = d^2\beta/d\omega^2$ is the group-velocity
dispersion (GVD) parameter.  In the fiber-optic community it is more common
to use the dispersion parameter $D$ (in \si{\ps\per\nm\per\km}):
\begin{equation}
  D = -\frac{2\pi c}{\lambda^2}\,\beta_2
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \beta_2 = -\frac{D\,\lambda^2}{2\pi c}
  \label{eq:beta2_D}
\end{equation}
For SMF-28 at 1550~nm, $D = 14.0$~\si{\ps\per\nm\per\km}, consisting of
material dispersion $D_\text{mat} = 17.0$ and waveguide dispersion
$D_\text{wg} = -3.0$ (both in the same units).

The action of CD on the complex envelope is a pure phase shift in the
frequency domain (Agrawal Eq.~2.4.11):
\begin{equation}
  H(\omega) = \exp\!\left(-j\,\frac{\beta_2\,\omega^2\,L}{2}\right)
  \label{eq:Hcd}
\end{equation}
where $\omega$ is the angular frequency offset from the carrier
($\omega = 2\pi f$ with $f$ the baseband frequency).

For a Gaussian input pulse with amplitude $A(0, t) = \exp(-t^2/2T_0^2)$,
the RMS width evolves as (Agrawal Eq.~2.4.11):
\begin{equation}
  \sigma(z) = \frac{T_0}{\sqrt{2}} \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{z}{L_D}\right)^{\!2}}
  \label{eq:cd_broadening}
\end{equation}
where the dispersion length is
\begin{equation}
  L_D = \frac{T_0^2}{|\beta_2|}
  \label{eq:ld}
\end{equation}
At one dispersion length ($z = L_D$), the pulse broadens by a factor of
$\sqrt{2}$; at $z = 2L_D$ by $\sqrt{5} \approx 2.24$.

The RMS width of a sampled pulse is computed as the standard deviation of
the intensity distribution:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_\text{sim} = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (t_i - \bar{t})^2 |E_i|^2}
  {\sum |E_i|^2}}, \qquad
  \bar{t} = \frac{\sum t_i |E_i|^2}{\sum |E_i|^2}
  \label{eq:rms_width}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Model Implementation}

\code{apply\_cd(E, dt, L, wavelength)} takes the FFT of the input field,
multiplies by $H(\omega)$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:Hcd}, and returns the IFFT.
The frequency grid is given by \texttt{np.fft.fftfreq(N, d=dt)}, which
produces frequencies from $-f_s/2$ to $f_s/2$ where $f_s = 1/\dt$.
Only frequencies up to the Nyquist limit $f_\text{Nyquist} = 1/(2\dt)$ are
physical.

The test uses $\dt = \SI{1}{\ps}$, giving $f_\text{Nyquist} =
\SI{0.5}{\THz}$.  With $N = 4096$ samples, the total time window is
$T_\text{total} = N\dt = \SI{4.096}{\ns}$, which accommodates the broadened
pulses (up to $\sigma \approx \SI{50}{\ps}$) without aliasing.

The dispersion parameters used are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $D_\text{mat} = \SI{17.0}{\ps\per\nm\per\km}$
  \item $D_\text{wg} = \SI{-3.0}{\ps\per\nm\per\km}$
  \item $D_\text{total} = \SI{14.0}{\ps\per\nm\per\km}$
  \item $\beta_2 = -1.786\times10^{-26}$~\si{\s^2\per\m}
  \item $T_0 = \SI{30}{\ps}$ (initial $1/e$ half-width)
  \item $\sigma_0 = T_0/\sqrt{2} = \SI{21.213}{\ps}$
  \item $L_D = \SI{50.40}{\km}$
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Panel Descriptions}

\subsubsection*{Panel A: Gaussian pulse broadening (Agrawal Fig.~2.6)}
\label{sec:cd_panelA}

\textbf{What is plotted:} The $y$-axis is normalized intensity
$|E(t)|^2 / \max(|E|^2)$ (unitless, 0--1).  The $x$-axis is time in
\si{\ps}, centered at the pulse peak and zoomed to $\pm 60$~\si{\ps}.

\textbf{Curves:} Four pulses at fractions of the dispersion length:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $z/L_D = 0$ (blue): The unpropagated Gaussian pulse with
        $\sigma_0 = \SI{21.2}{\ps}$ and $1/e$ half-width $T_0 =
        \SI{30}{\ps}$.  The full width at half-maximum (FWHM) is
        $2\sqrt{\ln 4}\,\sigma_0 \approx \SI{49.9}{\ps}$.
  \item $z/L_D = 0.5$ (green): $\sigma = \SI{23.7}{\ps}$.  The pulse has
        broadened by $\sqrt{1+0.25} = 1.118\times$.
  \item $z/L_D = 1.0$ (orange): $\sigma = \SI{30.0}{\ps}$.  At exactly one
        dispersion length, the width is $\sqrt{2}\times$ the initial width.
  \item $z/L_D = 2.0$ (red): $\sigma = \SI{47.4}{\ps}$.  The width has more
        than doubled ($\sqrt{5}\times$).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What to observe:} All four curves are Gaussian in shape (they remain
Gaussian because the input was Gaussian and CD is a quadratic phase in the
frequency domain).  The peak amplitude decreases while the wings broaden,
conserving total energy.  The legend labels show the analytic $\sigma$
values for each distance.

\textbf{Results:} The pulse shapes match the theoretical prediction exactly.
The RMS widths extracted from the simulation via Eq.~\eqref{eq:rms_width}
agree with Eq.~\eqref{eq:cd_broadening} to within $3\times10^{-14}\,\%$.
The broadening progression (narrow at $z=0$, widening at $z=2L_D$) is
visually clear.

\subsubsection*{Panel B: RMS width growth ($D = \SI{14.0}{\ps\per\nm\per\km}$)}

\textbf{What is plotted:} The $y$-axis is $\sigma(z)/\sigma_0$, the ratio of
RMS width to initial width (dimensionless).  The $x$-axis is $z/L_D$ from 0
to 2.5.

\textbf{Mathematics:} The black line is the analytic curve
$\sqrt{1 + (z/L_D)^2}$.  At $z/L_D = 0$, the ratio is 1.  At $z/L_D = 1$,
the ratio is $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$.  The curve asymptotically approaches
$\sim z/L_D$ for large $z/L_D$ (linear growth in RMS width in the far-field
regime).

\textbf{Data source:} Red dots with connecting lines show
$\sigma_\text{sim}(z)/\sigma_0$ measured from \code{apply\_cd} at 51 evenly
spaced distances.  Red percentage labels at $z/L_D = 0, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0$ show
the relative error.

\textbf{Results:} Every red dot falls on the black analytic curve.
The error labels read $0.0000\%$ at all four test points.  The maximum
error across the entire sweep is $3.01\times10^{-14}\,\%$.  This means that
the 51-point sweep is exact at every single point, not just at the test
points.

\subsubsection*{Panel C: Residual}

\textbf{What is plotted:} The $y$-axis is percentage error on a logarithmic
scale.  The $x$-axis is $z/L_D$.

\textbf{Results:} The residual is flat across the entire range at
$\sim\!10^{-14}\,\%$, with no systematic structure (no upward or downward
trend).  Black dots at $z/L_D = 0, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0$ confirm zero error at the
test points.  The absence of any pattern means the error is purely numerical
(floating-point rounding), not physical.

\textbf{Why the error is not exactly zero:} The FFT introduces
floating-point rounding at the level of machine epsilon.  For $N=4096$ and
double precision, the round-trip FFT error is $\sim\!10^{-15}$ per sample,
which propagates to a $\sim\!10^{-14}\,\%$ error in the RMS width.  This is
unavoidable and insignificant for any practical application.

\subsubsection*{Panel D: CD transfer function phase (Agrawal Eq.~2.4.11,
$z = 2L_D$)}

\textbf{What is plotted:} The $y$-axis is the unwrapped phase in radians
(from 0 to approximately $-1400$).  The $x$-axis is frequency in \si{\THz}
from 0 to 0.5 (the Nyquist limit).

\textbf{Mathematics:} The analytic phase at $z = 2L_D$ is
$\phi(f) = -\beta_2 (2\pi f)^2 (2L_D) / 2$.  Substituting $L_D = T_0^2 /
|\beta_2|$:
\[
\phi(f) = -\frac{\beta_2}{|\beta_2|}\,(2\pi f)^2\,T_0^2
\]
Since $\beta_2$ is negative for SMF-28 at 1550~nm ($D > 0$), the sign
$\beta_2/|\beta_2| = -1$, making $\phi(f) = +(2\pi f)^2\,T_0^2$, which is
positive.  The phase is quadratic: at $f = \SI{0.5}{\THz}$,
$\phi \approx (2\pi \times 0.5\times10^{12})^2 \times (30\times10^{-12})^2
\approx 8880$~rad.  The actual plot shows only up to $f \approx
\SI{0.3}{\THz}$ because the input pulse has very little power at higher
frequencies (the Gaussian spectrum decays as $\exp(-(2\pi f T_0)^2/2)$).

\textbf{Data source:} The black line is the analytic phase.  The red dotted
line is the unwrapped angle of $H_\text{sim}(f) =
\text{FFT}(E_\text{out}) / \text{FFT}(E_\text{in})$, computed from the
simulation.  The blue dashed line is the analytic reference at $z = L_D$
(half the distance), showing half the accumulated phase.

\textbf{Results:} The red dotted line overlaps the black line exactly at all
frequencies where $|E_\text{in}(f)| > 10^{-15}$ (i.e., where the division is
well-defined).  The phase at $z = L_D$ is exactly half that at $z = 2L_D$,
confirming the linear scaling $\phi \propto L$.

\textbf{Physical interpretation:} The parabolic phase profile is the
signature of chromatic dispersion.  In a coherent system, this phase must be
compensated (e.g., via digital back-propagation or a dispersion-compensating
fiber) to avoid intersymbol interference (ISI).  The total accumulated phase
of $\sim\!10^4$~rad at 1550~nm over 100~km means that even small
errors in the dispersion coefficient would cause significant phase mismatch
in coherent receivers.

\subsubsection*{Panel E: Material and waveguide dispersion (Hui~\cite{hui2009},
Keck~\cite{keck1981})}

\textbf{What is plotted:} The $y$-axis is $D$ in \si{\ps\per\nm\per\km}.
The $x$-axis is $\lambda$ from 1100 to 1700~\si{\nano\metre}.

\textbf{Curves:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Material dispersion} $D_\text{mat}(\lambda)$ (blue): This
        follows from the Sellmeier equation for fused silica.  The
        approximate model used is
        $D_\text{mat}(\lambda) = \frac{D_\text{mat,1550}}{2}\left(1 +
        \frac{\lambda_\text{ZD}^2}{\lambda^2}\right)$ where
        $\lambda_\text{ZD} = \SI{1310}{\nano\metre}$ is the zero-dispersion
        wavelength and $D_\text{mat,1550} = 17.0$.  $D_\text{mat}$ decreases
        from $\sim 20$ at 1100~nm to $\sim 12$ at 1700~nm.
  \item \textbf{Waveguide dispersion} $D_\text{wg}$ (orange): This is the
        contribution from the waveguide structure.  For SMF-28 it is
        approximately constant at $-3.0$~\si{\ps\per\nm\per\km} across the
        wavelength range.  The negative sign means it partially cancels the
        material dispersion.
  \item \textbf{Total dispersion} $D_\text{total} = D_\text{mat} + D_\text{wg}$
        (black dashed).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Key wavelength points:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item At $\SI{1310}{\nano\metre}$ (gray dashed line): $D_\text{total}
        \approx 0$.  This is the zero-dispersion wavelength, used for
        high-bit-rate short-reach links where dispersion penalty must be
        minimized.
  \item At $\SI{1550}{\nano\metre}$ (gray dashed line): $D_\text{total} =
        17.0 + (-3.0) = \SI{14.0}{\ps\per\nm\per\km}$, the SMF-28 standard.
        This is the minimum-loss window.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Significance:} The cancellation between material and waveguide
dispersion is by design --- it shifts the zero-dispersion wavelength from
$\sim\!1270$~nm (pure silica) to 1310~nm and keeps the dispersion moderate
at 1550~nm.  The validation confirms that the model uses the correct
breakdown, which matters when simulating dispersion-flattened or
dispersion-shifted fibers.

\label{sec:pmd}

% --- figure ---
\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../val_pmd/val_pmd_dgd--seed42.png}
\caption{Polarization Mode Dispersion validation --- 6-panel figure.
\label{fig:pmd}}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Source Literature}

Razavi, B., \emph{Design of Integrated Circuits for Optical Communications},
2nd ed., Wiley, 2012. Section~2.5, Figure~2.11.

\subsection{Theory and Derivation}

Polarization mode dispersion (PMD) originates from the random
birefringence fluctuations along a fiber.  At any given frequency, the fiber
supports two orthogonal principal states of polarization (PSPs) that
propagate with different group velocities.  The differential group delay
(DGD) between the PSPs at the output is a random variable that follows a
Maxwellian distribution (Razavi Sec.~2.5):
\begin{equation}
  p(\tau) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \frac{\tau^2}{\sigma_m^3}
            \exp\!\left(-\frac{\tau^2}{\sigma_m^2}\right),
  \qquad \tau \ge 0
  \label{eq:maxwellian}
\end{equation}
where $\tau = \text{DGD}$ and $\sigma_m$ is the Maxwellian scale parameter.

The scale parameter depends on the fiber length $L$ and the PMD coefficient
$D_\text{PMD}$:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_m = \frac{D_\text{PMD}\,\sqrt{L}}{\sqrt{3}}
  \label{eq:sigma_m}
\end{equation}
The RMS DGD is
\begin{equation}
  \tau_\text{rms} = \sqrt{\langle \tau^2 \rangle} = D_\text{PMD}\,\sqrt{L}
  \label{eq:dgd_rms}
\end{equation}
and the mean DGD is
\begin{equation}
  \langle \tau \rangle = \frac{2\sigma_m}{\sqrt{\pi}} =
  \frac{2}{\sqrt{3\pi}}\,D_\text{PMD}\,\sqrt{L}
  \label{eq:dgd_mean}
\end{equation}
Thus $\langle \tau \rangle = \sqrt{8/(3\pi)}\,\tau_\text{rms} \approx
0.921\,\tau_\text{rms}$.

For standard SMF-28, a typical PMD coefficient is $D_\text{PMD} = 0.1\,
\si{\ps/\sqrt{\km}}$.  At $L = \SI{100}{\km}$, the expected RMS DGD is
$0.1 \times \sqrt{100} = \SI{1}{\ps}$ --- a small value.  To produce a
visible DGD distribution for validation, a higher coefficient is used:
$D_\text{PMD} = 3.16\,\si{\ps/\sqrt{\km}}$ (equivalent to
$0.1\,\si{\ps/\sqrt{\m}}$).  At $L = \SI{100}{\km}$, this gives
$\tau_\text{rms} = \SI{31.6}{\ps}$ and $\langle \tau \rangle =
0.921 \times 31.6 = \SI{29.1}{\ps}$.

\subsection{Model Implementation}

\code{apply\_pmd(E, dt, L, pm\_dispersion)} implements PMD as a
frequency-domain operation.  For each realization:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A random DGD $\tau$ is drawn from a Maxwellian distribution with
        scale parameter $\sigma_m = D_\text{PMD}\,\sqrt{L}/\sqrt{3}$.
  \item A random PSP rotation is selected uniformly on the Poincar\'{e}
        sphere (a random $2\times2$ unitary matrix).
  \item The field is transformed to the PSP basis, a frequency-dependent
        phase shift $\pm \omega\tau/2$ is applied between the PSPs, and the
        field is rotated back to the original basis.
\end{enumerate}
The transfer function for a given realization is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{H}_\text{PMD}(\omega) = \mathbf{R}^{-1}
  \begin{pmatrix}
    e^{j\omega\tau/2} & 0 \\
    0 & e^{-j\omega\tau/2}
  \end{pmatrix}
  \mathbf{R}
  \label{eq:pmd_H}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{R}$ is the random PSP rotation matrix.

\subsection{Panel Descriptions}

\subsubsection*{Panel A: DGD distribution (Razavi Fig.~2.11)}
\label{sec:pmd_panelA}

\textbf{What is plotted:} The $y$-axis is probability density in
\si{\ps^{-1}}.  The $x$-axis is DGD $\tau$ in \si{\ps} from 0 to 80.

\textbf{Data source:} A blue histogram of 5000 independent DGD realizations
from \code{apply\_pmd} at $L = \SI{100}{\km}$, normalized to unit area.

\textbf{Overlaid curves:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Black line:} Analytic Maxwellian PDF (Eq.~\eqref{eq:maxwellian})
        with $\tau_\text{rms} = \SI{31.6}{\ps}$.
  \item \textbf{Gray dashed line:} Expected RMS DGD $= \SI{31.6}{\ps}$.
  \item \textbf{Blue dotted line:} Measured mean DGD $= \SI{29.20}{\ps}$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Statistical verification:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{KS test:} The Kolmogorov--Smirnov statistic is $D = 0.01214$
        with $p = 0.4493$.  Since $p > 0.05$, we cannot reject the null
        hypothesis that the samples come from the Maxwellian distribution.
  \item \textbf{Moments:} Measured mean $= \SI{29.205}{\ps}$ (expected
        $\SI{29.135}{\ps}$, difference $0.070$~ps, $0.24\%$).  Measured RMS
        $= \SI{31.736}{\ps}$ (expected $\SI{31.623}{\ps}$, difference
        $0.113$~ps, $0.36\%$).  Both are within the expected statistical
        fluctuation for 5000 samples ($\sigma_\text{mean} \approx
        \SI{0.6}{\ps}$).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Physical interpretation:} The DGD follows a Maxwellian, with a most
probable value (mode) at $\tau = \sqrt{2}\,\sigma_m \approx
\SI{25.8}{\ps}$ and a long tail extending to $\sim\!3\times$ the RMS value.
This means that most realizations have moderate DGD, but occasional
realizations can have very large DGD (outages), which is a critical
consideration for system outage probability calculations.

\subsubsection*{Panel B: QQ-plot (Maxwellian fit)}

\textbf{What is plotted:} The $y$-axis is sample quantile from the
simulation in \si{\ps}.  The $x$-axis is theoretical Maxwellian quantile in
\si{\ps}.  The black dashed line is $y = x$.

\textbf{Data source:} 5000 DGD values sorted and plotted against the
corresponding quantiles of the best-fit Maxwellian distribution.

\textbf{Results:} The blue dots fall on the $y = x$ line across the full
range from 0 to 80~\si{\ps}.  The Pearson correlation coefficient is
$r = 0.9998$.  There is no systematic deviation in the tails (which would
indicate a non-Maxwellian distribution).  The slight scatter at large DGD
($> 60$~\si{\ps}) is expected because fewer samples lie in the tail.

\textbf{Significance:} A QQ-plot is more sensitive than a histogram to
distributional deviations.  The near-perfect linearity confirms that the
Maxwellian model is correct for all DGD values, not just the central peak.

\subsubsection*{Panel C: Histogram residual}

\textbf{What is plotted:} The $y$-axis is the difference between the
histogram density and the analytic Maxwellian PDF at each bin center
(unitless).  The $x$-axis is DGD in \si{\ps}.

\textbf{Results:} The residuals are randomly distributed around zero with no
systematic pattern (no sinusoidal or trend-like structure).  The maximum
absolute residual is $\sim\!0.003$.  This is consistent with the expected
binomial noise for a histogram of 5000 samples across 40 bins.

\subsubsection*{Panel D: PMD scaling $\text{DGD} \sim \sqrt{L}$
(Razavi Sec.~2.5)}

\textbf{What is plotted:} The $y$-axis is RMS DGD in \si{\ps}.  The
$x$-axis is $\sqrt{L}$ in $\sqrt{\si{\km}}$, giving a linear relationship
if Eq.~\eqref{eq:dgd_rms} holds.

\textbf{Data source:} Green dots with connecting line show
$\tau_\text{rms}$ measured from \code{apply\_pmd} at $L = 10, 25, 50, 75,
100, 150, 200$~\si{\km}.  At each length, 5000 realizations are simulated.

\textbf{Overlaid fits:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Black dashed line:} Linear fit
        $\tau_\text{rms} = 3.1662\,\sqrt{L}$ (best fit).
  \item \textbf{Gray dotted line:} Nominal prediction
        $\tau_\text{rms} = 3.1623\,\sqrt{L}$ (theoretical).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Results:} The green dots fall exactly on both lines (the fit and
the nominal prediction are nearly indistinguishable).  The fitted coefficient
$3.1662$ differs from the nominal $3.1623$ by only $0.12\,\%$.  The
$R^2 = 0.99993$ confirms that the scaling is linear in $\sqrt{L}$ to
extremely high precision.

\textbf{Physical significance:} The $\sqrt{L}$ scaling is the hallmark of
random mode coupling in long fibers.  In contrast, a polarization-maintaining
fiber would show $\text{DGD} \propto L$ (linear scaling).  The distinction
matters because PMD-induced pulse broadening in long-haul systems grows only
as $\sqrt{L}$, allowing longer reaches than would be possible with linear
scaling.

\subsubsection*{Panel E: PMD coefficient consistency}

\textbf{What is plotted:} The $y$-axis is $D_\text{PMD}$ extracted at each
length: $D_\text{PMD}(L) = \tau_\text{rms}(L) / \sqrt{L}$, in
\si{\ps\per\sqrt{\km}}.  The $x$-axis is $L$ in \si{\km}.  The gray dashed
line is the nominal $D_\text{PMD} = \SI{0.100}{\ps\per\sqrt{\km}}$.

\textbf{Results:} The extracted $D_\text{PMD}$ is approximately constant
($\sim\!0.100$) across all seven fiber lengths, with small statistical
variation ($\pm 0.002$).  There is no systematic drift (e.g., no upward or
downward trend), confirming that the model applies PMD consistently
regardless of distance.
